\section{TempoRAC}
\label{sec:method}

Given a video of $T$ frames, we predict a non-negative density $\hat d(t)$
whose integral is the repetition count, $\hat c=\sum_t\hat d(t)$. \method
(\cref{fig:framework}) rests on one observation (\cref{sec:observation}): the
temporal sampling rate at which a frozen video encoder represents a repetition
should match its local tempo, which varies by a factor of thirty across videos
and drifts within them. We therefore (i) encode the video at several sampling
rates, (ii) process each rate with its own temporal expert, (iii) measure the
local tempo with a masked tempogram, and (iv) route each moment of the video to
the experts whose rate suits it, decoding the routed mixture once.

\subsection{Multi-rate token streams}
\label{sec:multirate}
A frozen video transformer $\mathcal{E}$ maps a clip of 16 frames to $8$
temporal tokens. For a sampling rate $s\in\mathcal{S}$ (frame stride inside the
clip) we slide clips spanning $16s$ frames with a hop of $4s$ frames, so each
token covers $2s$ frames and every token position is seen by four overlapping
clips, which we average. This yields, for each rate, a token sequence
$\mathbf{X}^{(s)}\in\R^{T_s\times P\times C}$ on its own grid of
$T_s=\lceil T/2s\rceil$ cells, where $P$ are spatial tokens. A small rate
represents a fast repetition with several tokens per cycle but sees only a short
context; a large rate sees several slow cycles inside one clip but blurs fast
ones (\cref{fig:observation}). All rates share the encoder, so the streams differ
only in temporal sampling.

\subsection{Rate experts}
For each rate, an attention pooling over the $P$ spatial tokens followed by a
temporal encoder $f_s$ (depthwise temporal convolution plus self-attention
blocks) produces responses $\mathbf{y}^{(s)}=f_s(\mathbf{X}^{(s)})$ on the
rate's own grid. Because $f_s$ has a fixed receptive field in cells, the same
architecture covers absolute periods that scale with $s$. A density head maps
each response to a rate-specific density $d_s$, which we bring to the finest
grid by repetition and division by $s$, preserving its mass.

\subsection{Local masked tempogram}
To decide which rate to trust at time $t$, we measure the local periodicity of
the finest-rate features $\mathbf{z}_t$ (unit-normalised, gradient-stopped).
For lags $\tau=1,\dots,L$ the masked local autocorrelation is
\begin{equation}
A(t,\tau)=\frac{\sum_u w(u-t)\,q_u q_{u+\tau}\,\langle\mathbf{z}_u,\mathbf{z}_{u+\tau}\rangle}
{\sum_u w(u-t)\,q_u q_{u+\tau}},
\label{eq:tempogram}
\end{equation}
where $w$ is a Hann window and $q$ masks padding. $A(t,\cdot)$ peaks at
multiples of the local period; together with its support it forms a
parameter-free, per-moment tempo descriptor. It is computed from one Gram
matrix and a gather, at negligible cost.

\subsection{Tempo routing and single decoding}
A router $r$ maps the tempogram (and, optionally, the expert context) to
simplex weights over rates, $\mathbf{g}(t)=\mathrm{softmax}(r(A(t,\cdot)))$,
smoothed over time. The output density is the routed mixture
\begin{equation}
\hat d(t)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S} g_s(t)\, d_s(t),\qquad \hat c=\sum_t \hat d(t).
\label{eq:mixture}
\end{equation}
Routing happens per moment, so a video whose pace changes can switch rates
within one sequence; decoding happens once, on the mixture, so overlapping
windows are never counted independently. RepNet~\cite{dwibedi2020repnet}
chooses a single stride per video at test time by a periodicity heuristic;
\method makes this choice local, learned and differentiable.

\subsection{Training objective}
Targets are unit-mass Gaussians centred on annotated cycles
($\sigma$ proportional to the cycle length), summed into $y(t)$. We minimise
two terms,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\lambda\,\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\sum_s g_s(t)\big(d_s(t)-y(t)\big)^2
+\frac{|\hat c-c|}{c+1}.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
The first is the competitive mixture-of-experts objective~\cite{jacobs1991adaptive}:
each rate expert is trained where the router sends it, and the router is pushed
towards the locally most accurate rate. The second is a normalised count error
on the routed mixture.
